Wireless RF Re-engineering

PRODUCTION 2026

A two-floor, 22-AP wireless estate where everything was slow and nobody could say why. I ran a formal RF survey first, which turned "the WiFi is bad" into numbers: 64 BSSIDs contending on one 2.4 GHz channel, transmit power high enough that cells bled through floors, and the whole DFS range sitting unused. Then I changed one class of variable at a time, each with a written user-impact assessment before the maintenance window.

Added Capacity
~12 DFS channels
Reported Issues

802.11ax 6 GHz DFS RF Survey Spectrum Analysis
Survey findings and what changed

Inter-floor attenuation measured between 2 and 41 dB depending on where you stood. The floors were not RF-isolated, but the power settings had been chosen as though they were, so floor one and floor two were competing for the same spectrum without anyone deciding they should.

2.4 GHz gives you three non-overlapping channels. There were 44 radios to spread across them, and the spread was badly lopsided: 64 BSSIDs on one channel, nine on another. Every client on the crowded channel queued behind traffic it had no interest in.

What changed:

  • Per-SSID band selection, so networks could be placed on the bands that suited them instead of every SSID appearing on every radio.
  • Flex radios reassigned to 6 GHz, halving the 2.4 GHz radio count from 44 to 22 and taking most of the contention with it.
  • DFS enabled, opening roughly 12 additional 5 GHz channels the estate had never used.
  • Transmit power cut from 28-30 dBm to 17-18 dBm, sizing cells against the measured attenuation.

One caveat. "Zero reported issues" is a proxy, not a measurement. It is the outcome users experienced, but the numbers I wanted, channel utilisation and client retry rate before and after, were never captured. Next survey, those get baselined first.

The IPsec failure taxonomy

Almost every tunnel problem I hit was one of three things. Writing them down as a checklist turned a day of guessing into twenty minutes of elimination.

MSS clamping. The tunnel comes up, ICMP works fine, then TCP hangs on anything with a real payload. Encapsulation overhead pushes packets past the path MTU, the don't-fragment bit does the rest, and the black hole looks like an application problem. Small packets always work, which is what makes it confusing.

NAT traversal. IKE begins negotiation on one port and relocates to another once NAT is detected. Anything stateful in the path that treats those as unrelated flows will tear down the association, intermittently.

Traffic-selector mismatch. Route-based and policy-based peers disagree about what a tunnel carries. One says "everything, let routing decide"; the other wants explicit subnet pairs. They negotiate happily and then pass no traffic at all.

A tunnel reporting "up" tells you nearly nothing. Control plane and data plane fail independently, so the health check has to prove a packet made the trip, not that the peers are talking.

What mesh radio teaches you about networking

Routing, MTU, retransmission and congestion control are all still there. What changes is that propagation, duty cycle and battery budget become constraints you cannot delegate, and the physical layer stops being something you assume works. After years of treating the cable as solved, that is a useful correction.

The silent authentication outage

The service would start cleanly, authenticate correctly for a few minutes, then stop. No crash, no error in the log, the process still running and still listening. It just stopped answering.

The cause was untuned LDAP connection pooling. Pooled connections sat idle past the directory's timeout and died, and because nothing tested a connection before handing it out, the server kept issuing dead handles to live requests. Authentication attempts went in and nothing came back.

Tuning the pool fixed it: connection reuse limits, lifetime, idle timeout, keepalive. The tuned values went into documentation as the baseline instead of surviving as folklore in one person's head.

A pooled connection is a cache, and every cache needs an explicit invalidation story. If you cannot say when an entry goes stale and what checks it before use, you have a silent-failure generator with good throughput numbers.

Automating the certificate lifecycle properly

Certificate renewal is where self-hosted services quietly rot. Someone renews by hand for a year, then changes team, and the service goes down on a Sunday ninety days later.

This one has no manual step anywhere in the chain: a DNS-01 challenge issues the certificate through the DNS provider's API, a deploy hook converts the result into the container format the service actually consumes, and the service reloads itself. Renewal is a scheduled job that nobody needs to remember, and the runbook records the command that proves it worked, not the expiry date it produced.

The conversion step is easy to skip. Plenty of services cannot read what your ACME client writes, so renewal succeeds, the client reports success, and the certificate lands in a format the service ignores. It keeps serving the old one until it expires. "The cron job ran" and "the service is presenting a valid certificate" are different claims, and only the second is worth monitoring.

Four things that cost me real time

A manifest carried a singular field name where the schema wanted the plural. Kubernetes accepted the object, discarded the field it did not recognise, and reported success. The config looked applied and did nothing for months, until something downstream made the absence visible. Strict schema validation catches this, because it rejects unknown fields instead of ignoring them.

A dangling reference to a deleted middleware took a host offline. Schema validation would not have caught it, since every file was individually valid and the problem only existed between them. There is now a small custom validator that resolves cross-resource references before anything merges, running in pre-commit alongside the linter and the schema check. Pre-commit is the enforcement point; CI runs the same checks advisory-only.

MetalLB excludes control-plane nodes from load-balancer eligibility by default. Sensible, right up until every node you own is a control plane and nothing can be assigned an address. The override is one flag. Working out that you need it took considerably longer.

Moving across several minor versions meant every component with an opinion about the API surface had to move too. I mapped the dependency ladder first, working out which ingress, certificate and GitOps controller versions were mutually compatible and compatible with the target. That turned a multi-day sprawl of discovering incompatibilities one at a time into a single planned window.

Benchmarking, and giving the GPU back

Prefill cost is not linear in context length. Pushing toward 33k tokens, time-to-first-token grows faster than the token count does, which makes context budget a latency decision as much as a memory one. I also measured prefix-cache behaviour, KV-cache footprint per model, and how the allocator hands out GPU-addressable memory, because the documented model and the observed behaviour did not agree.

The box is also a gaming machine, and both jobs want the same silicon. A systemd unit watches for a game launching and swaps the public model IDs over to CPU-only twins, so anything calling the API keeps working at reduced speed instead of failing. When the game exits, it swaps back. Nothing on the client side has to know.

There is a standing list of models evaluated and deferred, each with the reason and the condition that would justify revisiting. It costs a paragraph now and saves re-running the same evaluation in six months.

Where the docs were wrong, and what is still broken

I had assumed that preferring local intent handling meant the model only ever saw requests the built-in matcher could not resolve. The documentation reads that way. The implementation does not: the filter deciding which intents qualify is much narrower than its name suggests, and far more was reaching the model than I had designed for. Reading the pipeline code corrected the design. Re-reading the documentation would not have, because it was silent rather than wrong.

Tool-call reliability is still the rough edge. A small local model asked to hold a natural conversation and emit well-formed tool calls will do one of those well at a time, so choosing the model was mostly choosing which failure I could live with. It runs in production regardless, because an assistant that occasionally fumbles a tool call still beats one that ships every utterance in the house to somebody else. The runbook has not caught up with the deployment yet.

What the numbers said

Holding roughly 106 GiB of memory to produce 15.8 tokens per second is a bad trade when the same hardware runs a smaller model several times faster with capacity left over. Once that number existed, the decision made itself.

So I retired it deliberately instead of leaving it running. The runbook moved into an archive directory with a note recording what it did, what it measured, why it was shut down, and what would have to change for it to be worth revisiting.

A PoC that is quietly abandoned teaches nobody anything. Six months later someone proposes it again from first principles, usually me, and pays the same cost to reach the same conclusion.

Two design decisions worth the argument

The obvious design pipes HR data through a spreadsheet that somebody maintains. That makes a human the integrity guarantee, and humans go on holiday. Reading directly from the HR system with a read-only key removes the intermediary, so the automation and the source of truth agree by construction. The failure mode changes from "the sheet is stale", which is silent and can persist for months, to "the API is unreachable", which is loud and gets fixed today.

Mail forwarding expires long after the account is archived. Filing that cleanup ticket immediately means it sits open in the queue for two months, poisoning every ticket-age and time-to-resolution figure the team reports. So the job keeps its own queue and files the ticket when it becomes actionable. Automation that corrupts your metrics costs you something even when the work it performs is correct, because the numbers are how the team gets resourced.

Safety properties

The vendor's usage report lags reality by enough to matter. Enumerating accounts live costs more API calls and returns a true number. The gap between the two was wide enough to have caused a wrong purchase.

Filtering out terminated hires sounds correct until you find that a rescinded offer flips to terminated while keeping its future start date, so a deny-list passes it through and forecasts a seat for somebody who is never arriving. Allow-listing the two statuses that mean "starting" is the same amount of code without that hole.

Zero employees back from the HR system means a broken query. The job exits non-zero instead of forecasting against nothing. Every implausible input needs a defined refusal.

All per-person reduction happens inside the query layer, so no individual's data reaches a shell variable, a log line, or a ticket body. The output is a count, which is all anything downstream needs.
